## Compare duties, not labels

Public bodies often use similar words for materially different acts. Approval may mean permission to proceed, permission to charge a rate, acceptance into a procurement path, adoption of a technical method, or authority to offer service. Cross-case learning begins by identifying the exact instrument, decision maker, delegation, duty, discretion, and affected right.

An institution cannot borrow another institution’s authority merely because both records are called approvals.

## Separate permission from obligation

Some decisions permit activity; others compel it, fund it, condition it, monitor it, or define how claims must be tested. The transfer review records whether the decision maker had a duty to act, a range of lawful options, a public-notice obligation, an appeal path, and authority to impose safeguards or remedies.

Those features shape what can be learned. A transparent rationale may travel as a governance practice even when the underlying legal power cannot.

## Preserve accountability structure

The comparison must retain conflicts, recusal, dissent, affected-party participation, reasons, conditions, sunset, and review. A faster decision is not automatically a better decision if speed resulted from narrower participation, weaker evidence duties, or fewer remedy rights.

Phase 76 also asks whether authority and implementation were separated. The institution selecting an option may not own delivery, monitor performance, or compensate harmed parties.

## Administrative reuse needs a bounded receipt

A template, checklist, hearing practice, or monitoring clause may be reusable across authorities. The bounded reuse decision states which part travels, why it is compatible, what must be adapted, where it is prohibited, and who accepted responsibility for the new context.

No analogy, precedent, or prior success can silently create present authority.
